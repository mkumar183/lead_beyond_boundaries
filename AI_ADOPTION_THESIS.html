Project Report & Thesis

Leading the AI Transition as an Adaptive Challenge

Applying the Lead Beyond Boundaries framework to AI adoption across an engineering organisation

Programme: Lead Beyond Boundaries (Syngenta · Purple Cow Consulting)
Domain: Engineering / AI Adoption at Scale
Method: Principle-driven action research
Status: Field-tested · reusable playbook
Workshop: Feb 2026
Report date: June 2026

Abstract

Most enterprise AI adoption efforts fail not because the technology is immature, but because leaders treat an adaptive challenge as a technical one. They provision licences, mandate usage, and measure logins — and then join the ~85% of organisations whose AI pilots never reach production.

This report documents an alternative: an attempt to lead AI adoption across an engineering organisation strictly through the principles of the Lead Beyond Boundaries leadership framework. We frame AI adoption as eight interlocking adaptive challenges across three groups — a Human Shift (new skills, the speed of change, and job-loss fear), a Reality Gap (unclear productivity, a shifted SDLC bottleneck, and hype obscuring real machine learning), and a Responsibility Gap (ethics, security, and the principle that accountability remains human even when the agent writes the code). We then apply each model in the workbook — Heifetz's adaptive/technical distinction, the Loyal-Soldier-to-Growth-Partner mindshift, the VALUE question discipline, the Trust model, the Three Levels of Listening, and Non-Linear Thinking — as a concrete leadership move against a specific challenge. The output is a reusable, twelve-step playbook and a set of institutionalised practices (a Coding Agents Policy, an AI-disclosure norm, and a recurring cross-team Brown Bag forum) that converts adoption from a one-time rollout event into a sustained leadership practice.

1Context & Motivation

The central thesis of the Lead Beyond Boundaries workbook is that most leadership challenges today are adaptive, not technical — they cannot be commanded through; they must be mobilised. AI adoption is the perfect contemporary test of that thesis.

By 2026, coding agents (Claude Code, Cursor, GitHub Copilot, and others) had become widely available across the organisation — spanning multiple products, dozens of squads, and several product groups. The tools were no longer the constraint. The constraint was human: identity, fear, skill, trust, and habit. Industry benchmarks at the time told a consistent story — roughly 78% of enterprises were running AI pilots, but under 15% reached production. The gap between pilot and production was not an engineering gap; it was a leadership gap.

This project asked a single question: If we lead AI adoption using the workbook's principles rather than the default management reflexes, what changes — in the moves we make, and in the outcomes we get? The report is structured to show the delta between the two approaches at every step.

2The Adoption Problem: Eight Challenges in Three Groups

Through structured listening with engineers, engineering managers, and product partners, we found that "AI adoption is hard" decomposes into seven distinct challenges. Grouping them clarifies why a generic rollout fails — each group requires a different leadership response.

Group A — The Human Shift (identity-level)

  1. A completely new skill, not a tool upgrade. AI adoption is not "use Copilot to write code faster." It is learning prompt engineering — a fundamentally different craft. Engineers must still understand systems and design deeply, but they no longer write the specific syntax themselves. This is a seismic identity shift, not a training-video fix.
  2. The speed of change creates fear. Models and tools evolve faster than people can adapt. What is valid today may be obsolete in months. The velocity itself — not merely the technology — is what breeds anxiety and paralysis. People cannot find stable ground to stand on.
  3. Psychological safety is under threat. Engineers fear losing their jobs. The future of the software engineer feels genuinely uncertain, and uncertainty paralyses rather than motivates.

Group B — The Reality Gap (why the value is unclear)

  1. Real productivity benefits are unclear — and AI hallucinates. AI generates code quickly, but it also produces confident, wrong output. Testing, validating, and ensuring reliability remain slow. The net gain is murky; engineers who try AI and find the promise oversold become the hardest skeptics to re-engage.
  2. The bottleneck has shifted, not disappeared. Writing code became dramatically faster — but testing, code review, deployment, and observability did not. A system is only as fast as its slowest step, and the slowest step is no longer the one AI accelerated.
  3. Hype has hidden the real work. Generative-AI / LLM hype has obscured that real machine learning — supervised and unsupervised — is where much of the durable value lives. Most engineers were never trained to work with data, so they cannot yet leverage AI beyond chat-style coding help. We are adopting the hype before building the data literacy.

Group C — The Responsibility Gap (risk we cannot ignore)

  1. Ethical AI and security threats. Bias, transparency, and fairness are now engineering concerns, not legal footnotes. AI also introduces new attack surfaces — data leakage, prompt injection, IP exposure, and unvetted dependencies. The real ask is to move fast and stay responsible.
  2. Accountability stays human, even when the agent writes the code. This is the single most critical — and most easily forgotten — factor of the whole transition. An AI agent can generate, refactor, test, and even raise a pull request, but it cannot be accountable for the outcome. When an AI-authored change causes an incident, leaks data, or ships a defect, the responsibility does not transfer to the model or its vendor; it remains squarely with the engineer who accepted the work, the manager who approved it, and the sponsor who owns the system. The danger is a diffusion of responsibility: as the machine does more of the visible work, humans subtly feel less ownership of the result — "the AI wrote it" becomes an implicit excuse. Left unaddressed, this erodes the review discipline, the testing rigour, and the sense of craftsmanship that quality depends on. Accountability must therefore be made explicit and non-transferable: AI is a tool in the workflow, never a name on the accountability chain.
"Adaptive challenges can only be addressed through changes in people's priorities, beliefs, habits, and loyalties. Making progress requires going beyond any authoritative expertise to mobilise discovery, shedding certain entrenched ways, tolerating losses, and generating the new capacity to thrive anew." — Ronald Heifetz

3Diagnosis: Adaptive vs Technical

Heifetz's distinction is the project's diagnostic lens. The first — and most consequential — leadership act is to correctly classify the problem. A technical problem has a known solution applicable with existing expertise; an adaptive problem requires new learning and behaviour change. Misclassifying an adaptive problem as technical is the root cause of pilot purgatory.

ChallengeThe technical (wrong) readingThe adaptive (correct) reading
New skill"Give them a tool and a tutorial."Prompt engineering is a new way of thinking — learn to direct, not write.
Speed of change"Keep buying the latest tool."Stabilise principles and guardrails while tools churn beneath them.
Job fear"Reassure them in a town hall."Job uncertainty is rational; it must be designed for, not dismissed.
Productivity"Count lines generated."Hallucination and validation consume the gains; measure real outcomes.
Bottleneck"Celebrate faster coding."The constraint moved to testing, review, deploy — follow it.
Hype / data"Roll out more LLM tools."A data-literacy gap, not a licensing gap — invest in ML fundamentals.
Ethics / security"Legal will handle it later."New habits in every engineer, embedded from day one.
Accountability"The AI generated it — so the tool owns the risk."Accountability is non-transferable; AI is never on the chain. Engineer → EM → sponsor.
Method noteThe diagnosis is not academic. Each row dictates a different intervention. The remainder of this report shows how the workbook's models supply those interventions.

A complementary lens from the workbook is the Dance vs Balcony distinction. Leading AI adoption requires constant movement between the dance (engaging in the day-to-day pilots and code) and the balcony (stepping back to see the patterns — which fears are repeating, where energy is draining, which bottleneck has moved). A leader stuck on the dance floor mistakes activity for progress; a leader stuck on the balcony loses credibility with the engineers doing the work.

To make the delta visible, we first articulated how the organisation would default to leading this change without the framework — the Loyal Soldier reflexes the workbook names. These are well-intentioned but adaptive-blind.

5Conceptual Framework: The Principles as Leadership Moves

The framework is not a menu of slogans; it is a sequence of disciplines, each answering a specific challenge. The following sub-sections take each model from the workbook and show the concrete move it produced.

5.1 · The Mindshift: Loyal Soldier → Growth Partner

The framework does not change the goal (adopt AI well); it changes the leadership stance. This is the pivot on which every later move depends.

From: Loyal SoldierTo: Growth PartnerApplied to AI adoption
Respond to expectationsAnticipate & engageTreat prompt engineering as a career shift; design learning paths ahead of demand.
Comply and appeaseCreate & collaborateCo-design the future role with engineers, not for them.
Communicate at task levelsCommunicate impactMake the case through purpose and outcomes, not mandate and logins.
Wait to be invitedInfluence by designBuild cross-squad practice and measurement deliberately, not reactively.

5.2 · The VALUE Discipline

VALUE converts "do AI" into a question discipline. Each letter is a deliberate question asked before acting, and each maps to a predictable failure point.

V — Visualise

Anchor: "If this succeeds beyond expectations, what is different in 3–5 years?"

The Visualise question reframes the entire effort. Three to five years out, the rigid boundaries dissolve: a "frontend" or "backend" engineer becomes a full-stack engineer cutting across dev, QA, DevOps, and data, able to tie a whole solution together because AI supplies the depth in any single layer. Software becomes dramatically cheaper to build — a two-week feature becomes a two-day feature — which raises a strategic question the organisation must answer: are we ready for 10× more ideas reaching production, or 10× fewer engineers shipping the same roadmap? The durable differentiator shifts from "can you code this" to "do you understand the business well enough to choose what to build?" Engineers move from order-takers to growth partners alongside product management. The implication: career ladders, hiring rubrics, and the very definition of "senior" must be rewritten, or we will keep promoting for a craft the market no longer pays for.

A — Anticipate & Ask

Anchor: "What patterns are we normalising? What question are we afraid to ask?"

Anticipation catches the "pilot becomes permanent pilot" pattern early, and names the unspoken question: "If AI writes 60% of the code, what happens to the engineer who built their identity on writing it?" It also anticipates the predictable productivity-disillusionment wave — teams typically hit it around week 6–8, when they discover that testing and validation still consume the time AI freed in coding — and prepares a nuanced narrative before that disappointment arrives.

L — Lead from Purpose

Anchor: "What is the cause I am serving beyond my role or title?"

The cause must be reframed away from an "efficiency mandate" (which reads as "headcount cut" and triggers defence) toward "engineers as growth partners — closer to the business outcome." The test of the message: would the most skeptical senior engineer in the room recognise themselves as the beneficiary of the change, or its casualty?

U — Understand Context & Cultural Nuance

Anchor: "Am I speaking the language of my stakeholder, or expecting them to learn mine?"

The same capability must be translated per audience — for a domain expert ("this changes how a recommendation gets made"), for a PM ("this collapses discovery-to-prototype from weeks to days"), and for an engineer ("this is your pair, not your replacement"). Crucially, Understand also means cutting through the hype honestly: naming that generative AI is one tool, not all of AI, and that durable value often sits in real ML on the organisation's own data. In a hype-saturated room, speaking plainly about what AI is and is not is itself a trust-building act.

E — Enable, Energise & Engage

Anchor: "What is making it hard for others to do their best work? What needs to be removed, not added?"

Enablement is where the Reality and Responsibility gaps are closed. Concretely: publish guardrails — including ethics and security — before pushing usage, removing the "is AI OK here?" ambiguity that silently blocks adoption. Build the missing data literacy by investing in data and ML fundamentals, not just prompt tips, so teams can move beyond chat-style help to durable value. Name the bottleneck shift explicitly and invest in AI-assisted testing, automated review, and CI/CD acceleration — because speeding one stage of a pipeline without the rest creates a traffic jam, not a breakthrough. Finally, make the productivity conversation honest: measure what improved and what didn't, because inflated claims destroy the very trust adoption depends on.

5.3 · The Trust Model — Building Psychological Safety

Engineers cannot adopt what they fear will replace them. The Trust model (Reliability · Acceptance · Openness · Congruence) treats trust as the prerequisite of adoption, not its by-product.

  • Reliability — Publish a Coding Agents Policy (including ethics and security guardrails) and stick to it. Models already evolve too fast for engineers to feel stable; the one thing leaders can stabilise is the framework. If you say "no one loses their job because of AI adoption," that statement must hold.
  • Acceptance — When a senior engineer says "AI is wrong half the time," they are simultaneously right (it hallucinates) and protecting a decade of invested craft. "My role won't exist in two years" is not resistance — it is a rational reading. Acceptance means making space for the fear, not dismissing it as negativity. This is the workbook's candour with respect.
  • Openness — Be honest about the hype and the hallucination; be transparent that workflows will change. Share what you do not know — "we don't know exactly how roles will evolve, but we will design that evolution with you." Brown Bag sessions surface what didn't work, not only the wins.
  • Congruence — Leaders who advocate AI must use it visibly themselves, including struggling with it in public. If a leader preaches adoption but shows no AI learning time on their own calendar, the gap between word and action is visible — and trust collapses.
Capstone moveCo-author the AI usage guidelines with the loudest skeptic in each squad. People defend what they build — so we replaced conversion attempts with co-authoring, turning "not adopting is not a choice" from a mandate into a movement.

5.4 · Three Levels of Listening

Most adoption-discovery conversations stop at Level 1 — the stated objection. The real challenges only surface at Levels 2 and 3. The table below shows what each level revealed.

LevelWhat we heardWhat it actually was
0 · My assumptions"Engineers resist because they're behind on AI."A leadership bias — the ask was poorly framed, not the people deficient.
1 · What is stated"AI hallucinates." · "I still test everything." · "Tools change monthly."The presenting problem — real, but the surface.
2 · Feelings & needs"Will my manager think I didn't do the work?" · "15 years mastering Java — what was it for?"Identity grief and fear of being seen as replaceable — the real blocker.
3 · What is possible"If you owned the AI quality guidelines, what would they look like?"The question that converted skeptics into authors.
FindingEvery institutionalised practice in this project — the Coding Agents Policy, the AI-disclosure norm, the Brown Bag forum, the SDLC-bottleneck redesign — emerged from Level 2 and Level 3 listening, not from a leadership decree.

5.5 · Non-Linear Thinking — The Weekly Cadence

Adoption is not an event you launch; it is a cadence you run. The 4 A's became a weekly leadership practice that keeps the team ahead of a field that moves faster than quarterly planning.

  • Ask — What is each pilot owner not telling us in the status? And specifically, "where is the bottleneck now?" — naming a constraint before it normalises.
  • Anticipate — Which pilots hit identity-threat resistance in the next 30 days? Which model update invalidates current workflows this quarter? Which use case carries an ethics or security risk to catch early?
  • Amplify — Spotlight the agents that shipped to production, but also amplify the engineers building the new skill — prompt engineering, data literacy, PM partnership. Amplify the new identity, not just the new tool.
  • Align — Hold alignment conversations with sponsors before the decision moment; align on what "productivity" means before dashboards are built; align with HR on the new career path before engineers ask.

6What We Actually Did — The Interventions

Principles only matter if they change behaviour. This section documents the concrete artefacts and practices the framework produced — the "project" part of the report.

6.1 · Diagnosis & reframing (Heifetz · Mindshift)

We explicitly reclassified AI adoption from a tooling rollout to an adaptive challenge, and named the eight challenges in a shared vocabulary the whole organisation could use. This single reframing changed the success metric from "% logged in" to "rework reduced and reliable outcomes shipped," and shifted leadership posture from waiting for a mandate to anticipating and designing.

6.2 · The Coding Agents Policy — guardrails first (VALUE-E · Trust-Reliability)

Rather than push usage and write rules after the first incident, we published a Coding Agents Policy before broad usage. It standardised tool choice per team (removing decision fatigue and ambiguity), embedded ethics and security expectations from day one, and established a clear accountability chain — engineer → engineering manager → sponsor — with AI never on the accountability chain. Human-in-the-loop review gates were defined for any AI-assisted change reaching production.

6.3 · The AI-disclosure norm (Trust-Openness · Listening L3)

A Level-3 question — "what would make you feel safe enough to experiment publicly?" — produced a lightweight norm for disclosing AI assistance in pull requests. This removed the hidden fear ("will my manager think I didn't do the work?") that was silently suppressing experimentation, and made AI use a normal, visible, discussable part of the workflow rather than a quiet embarrassment.

6.4 · The Brown Bag forum — cross-squad learning (Hofstede-collectivism · Trust-Openness)

We established a recurring, cross-team Brown Bag forum whose agenda explicitly includes what did not work, not only success stories. Failure content is the trust-builder; in a collectivist culture, learning spreads through the group, not the lone champion. The forum became the channel through which new agents and prompting techniques propagated across squads.

6.5 · Co-authoring with skeptics (Trust-Acceptance)

The most senior, most vocal skeptic in each squad was invited to co-author that squad's usage boundaries. Because people defend what they build, this converted the highest-risk resisters into the strongest local advocates — and produced more conservative, more trusted guardrails than leadership would have written alone.

6.6 · Designing for the whole SDLC (VALUE-E · 4 A's)

Acknowledging that the bottleneck had moved downstream, investment was deliberately steered beyond code-generation toward AI-assisted testing, automated review, and CI/CD acceleration — treating the SDLC as a pipeline to be balanced rather than a single stage to be sped up.

6.7 · The weekly 4 A's cadence (Non-Linear Thinking)

Leadership adopted a weekly Ask / Anticipate / Amplify / Align rhythm to stay ahead of model releases, identity-threat flare-ups, and the predictable disillusionment wave — institutionalising adoption as an ongoing practice rather than a launch.

7Findings & Evidence

The interventions produced both quantitative signals and durable structural change. The most instructive result is qualitative: the team's behaviour shifted from reacting to anticipating.

7.1 · Quantitative signals from pilots

A representative pilot — a React Native major-version upgrade — illustrates the "AI for speed, humans for correctness" pattern the framework encouraged.

The test-quality comparison is the more important finding: naïve AI test generation reached 78% coverage but with a 30% false-positive rate (judged "never use"), whereas a human-led three-stage AI flow reached 82% coverage with only 5% false positives (judged "best"). The squad explicitly chose the safer path — evidence that the "measure honestly" discipline (VALUE-E) changed real engineering decisions, not just rhetoric.

FindingAI delivered genuine speed gains only when paired with human-owned correctness. Unsupervised AI output failed the team's own quality bar. This directly validates the Reality-Gap framing: productivity is real but conditional, and the condition is human judgement.

8Discussion

The training's largest gift was not a tool — it was a shared vocabulary that makes the invisible visible. "Anticipate and ask" gave the team permission to name the question they were afraid to ask. "Invisible Influencer" identified the quiet senior engineer whose adoption was the real unlock. "Growth Partner" gave language to a stance that previously had no name.

Three tensions surfaced and are worth recording for anyone reusing this playbook:

  • Honesty vs momentum. Admitting that productivity gains are conditional and that AI hallucinates risks slowing enthusiasm. We found the opposite: candour built the trust that sustained adoption, while inflated claims created the skeptics that stalled it.
  • Stability vs currency. Engineers need stable ground, but the tools change monthly. Resolving this meant stabilising the principles and guardrails while letting the tools churn beneath them — separating what must hold steady from what is allowed to move.
  • Speed vs correctness. The pilots showed that the value of AI is unlocked precisely at the human-judgement layer. Leaders who optimise for raw generation speed will degrade quality; those who position humans as the correctness owners capture the gain safely.
  • Automation vs accountability. The more the agent does, the easier it is for humans to feel they own the result less — a quiet diffusion of responsibility. We countered this by making the accountability chain explicit and non-transferable (engineer → EM → sponsor, with AI never on it), and by treating the human-in-the-loop review gate not as bureaucracy but as the moment where ownership is consciously re-asserted. The lesson: delegating the work is fine; delegating the responsibility is not — and the two must be deliberately kept separate.

Limitations

The quantitative evidence is drawn from early pilots and should be read as directional, not definitive. Cultural factors (high power distance, high uncertainty avoidance, collectivism) shaped which interventions worked; organisations with different cultural profiles should re-derive the specific moves from the same principles rather than copying the tactics wholesale.

10Conclusion

The technology will keep changing — every quarter, every model release. What will not change are the human challenges underneath: the identity shift, the fear, the unclear value, the moving bottleneck, the hype, and the responsibility. This is precisely why a leadership framework — rather than a tooling strategy — is the durable asset.

The project demonstrates the workbook's core thesis in a live, high-stakes setting: AI adoption is an adaptive challenge, and treating it as one changes both the moves leaders make and the outcomes they achieve. The deliverable is not a one-time plan but a reusable practice — a playbook any leader, in any squad, can pick up and run. And as the field continues to accelerate, the central conviction holds in both directions: not adopting AI is not a choice — but mandating it is not a strategy. The space between those two truths is exactly where adaptive leadership lives, and it is what separates the 15% who reach production from the 85% who do not.
