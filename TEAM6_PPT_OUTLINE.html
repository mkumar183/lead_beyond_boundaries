4 How the training helped

Three specific contributions — pick the language of the workbook deliberately.
Mindshift Heifetz adaptive-vs-technical frame let us name what was happening. "Loyal Soldier → Growth Partner" became a shared vocabulary on how to engage stakeholders. We stopped asking "what does leadership want us to do?" and started asking "what does this organisation need us to do, and how do we mobilise it?"
Harder problems — the subtle / adaptive space Before: we treated AI adoption as a tooling rollout problem. After: we see it as an identity / craft / habit shift. The workbook gave us a lens to see the "unspoken but influential" forces — senior engineers' identity threat, junior fear of looking lazy, EM concern about delivery risk. We can now name them.
More structured VALUE framework as a question discipline (Visualise → Anticipate → Lead from Purpose → Understand Context → Enable). Trust Model breaks "build trust" into 4 measurable moves. Stakeholder map turns "stakeholder management" into a drawable artefact. Three Levels of Listening turns conversations into deliberate practice.
Changes we made — the leadership moves themselves

5 Growth Partner mindshift — what we said yes to, and what we said no to

Said NO to (Loyal Soldier defaults):

  • Waiting to be told what to pilot
  • Communicating at task level ("we ran the demo, here are the metrics")
  • Compliance reporting ("X% of teams have onboarded")

Said YES to (Growth Partner moves):

  • Anticipating what will block adoption 6 months out — and acting now
  • Communicating impact ("this changes how a grower gets their seed recommendation")
  • Influencing by design — Stakeholder Map before town-hall
  • Bringing imperfect artefacts to be co-edited, not polished decks to be approved

How we are overcoming the shift (this is harder than the framing):

  • Naming the resistance in ourselves, not just in others. Loyal Soldier defaults — wait for the brief, complete the brief, report up — feel safe. The training gave us a vocabulary to spot when we are reaching for them and pause. The first move is internal, not external.
  • Public commitments to "Growth Partner moves" in 1:1s with our leaders. Naming what we will do differently and inviting accountability is itself a Growth Partner act. Without that ritual, the new behaviour does not stick.
  • Sharing in-flight work, not just shipped work. Brown Bag, draft documents (this deck), the Agent-discussion cadence in Planting — we are practising "imperfect-artefact-shared-early" as a default. Repetition is the only thing that converts framing into habit.
  • Accepting that the shift is partial, not total. The training does not erase Loyal Soldier mode; it gives us a choice. The honest learning is that we still default to it in some rooms — and we keep noticing.
Say: "The clearest tell: a year ago we would have polished this deck for two weeks before showing you. Today, we are showing you the working draft and inviting you in. That is the mindshift. The hard part is doing it consistently — that is the work in progress."

6 How the V-A-L-U-E framework shaped our approach — letter by letter

All five letters, with the move each one produced for this initiative.

V — Visualise. Question: "If this succeeds beyond expectations, what is different 3–5 years from now?" Move it produced: connecting AI adoption to grower outcomes, not developer productivity alone. Without V, our story stops at engineering hours saved. With V, every pilot has a line of sight to the field — faster software → better agronomic tools → better grower decisions.

A — Anticipate & Ask. Question: "What patterns are repeating that we might be normalising? What question are we afraid to ask?" Move it produced: catching the "pilot becomes permanent pilot" pattern early; treating senior-engineer identity threat as a named, addressable risk rather than as unexplained foot-dragging. Did A really help? Yes — concretely: the Planting Agent-discussion cadence (P3★) exists because Chitra asked the anticipate question with Ram. Without it, Planting would have shipped one agent and stopped. With it, they shipped two and are designing more.

L — Lead from Purpose. Question: "What is the cause I am serving beyond my role or title?" Move it produced: stops us framing AI adoption as "efficiency mandate" (which reads as headcount threat) and reframes as "honour what these tools are actually for — grower decisions." Reframing matters because the same words sound different from "executive ask" vs "purpose statement."

U — Understand Context & Cultural Nuance. Question: "Am I speaking the language of my stakeholder? Where might my intent be correct but my impact misaligned?" Move it produced: stopped translating "this LLM does X" into engineering rooms; started saying "this changes how an agronomist gets a seed recommendation." Same change, audience-matched.

E — Enable, Energise & Engage. Question: "What is making it hard for stakeholders to do their best work? What needs to be removed, not added?" Move it produced: published guardrails (Coding Agents Policy) before pushing adoption, so engineers know exactly when AI is encouraged vs exception-required. Removing the "is AI OK here?" ambiguity unblocked more usage than any training video would have.

Say: "VALUE is not a slogan. It is a question discipline. Each letter is a question we now ask before we move — and each has changed a specific decision we would have made differently."

7 Identifying champions and finding the heavy users

Champions are the multiplier. We do not pick them — we recognise them.

How we identify champions (the method, not the wish list):

  • Cross-reference three signals — (a) tools-access records (who requested and uses Claude / Cursor / Copilot most actively per Coding Agents Policy data), (b) public artefacts produced (Confluence pages, PRs, brown-bag sessions), (c) peer mentions in 1:1s ("X showed me this"). Anyone strong on all three is a real champion; strong on only one is a candidate.
  • Trust the Invisible Influencer per squad. The workbook's Hofstede-collectivism move — every squad has one senior engineer whose quiet adoption is the unlock. Find them before running a town hall.
  • Distinguish "AI-curious" (lots of talk) from "heavy user" (lots of shipped artefacts). The latter is what we recruit; the former are next-tier candidates.

8 Psychological safety — how we are making it real, not slogan

Three concrete practices, mapped to workbook concepts:

  • Trust → Acceptance. When a senior engineer says "AI gives wrong suggestions half the time," we don't argue. We accept the position — it is protecting years of invested craft — before trying to move it. The skeptic is the signal, not the obstacle.
  • Hofstede Uncertainty Avoidance handled deliberately. Engineers won't experiment without guardrails. So we published guardrails first — Coding Agents Policy (pick exactly one of Claude Code / Cursor / Copilot), AI usage disclosure norms — before pushing usage.
  • Skeptic ownership. The AI usage guideline document gets co-authored with the loudest skeptic in each squad. People defend what they build. Conversion attempts are replaced with co-authoring.
Say: "Psychological safety isn't a vibe. It's that engineers know exactly where the boundary is between encouraged and exception-required — and the loudest skeptic helped write that boundary."

9 Hallucination and accountability — how we address them, with named guardrails

"AI for Speed. Humans for Correctness." — codified across Syngenta, not just our deck.

The principle the whole org has codified, applied to our work:

  • "No AI system shall make autonomous decisions on sensitive business data without human oversight" — verbatim from the [DRAFT] Syngenta Integrated Agentic Reference Architecture. Treated as a non-negotiable.
  • "AI for Speed. Humans for Correctness." — the Agrobiodiversity squad's published operating principle (Lalitkumar Sakare, P5). Human defines scenarios → AI drafts → Human validates.
  • "AI without guardrails makes it worse — we inherit tech debt at machine speed." — verbatim from the 2026 AI-Driven Coverage initiative. The line that anchors why guardrails come before usage.

The named guardrail layers we rely on (not invented for this deck — already in the org):

  • Portkey LLM Guardrails — the org's "AI Compliance Checkpoint" for every model call. Aligns to the AI Manifesto's Human Oversight commitment.
  • 8-pillar guardrail framework (per RFC-009 Data Platform AI Strategy) — explicitly includes jailbreak detection, compliance checks, and hallucination detection as named pillars.
  • Coding Agents Policy — engineers pick exactly one of Claude Code / Cursor / Copilot. Ambiguity is itself a hallucination risk; one-tool-one-team removes it.
  • Human-in-the-loop by design — for AI-Driven Coverage: "Generated tests never merge without developer review." For TouchStone: explicit agent boundaries + human gates. For Planting agents: PR-based review for every fix.
  • Three-stage test workflow (per Agrobiodiversity doc): naive AI test generation = 30% false positives — "never use". Human-led AI three-stage workflow = 5% false positives. The squad explicitly chose the safer path.

Accountability chain we make explicit on every AI artefact:

  • Engineer who reviewed/merged it — accountable for correctness
  • EM whose squad shipped it — accountable for the team's overall quality
  • Sponsor (typically Manoj-org for these pilots) — accountable for the priority being served
  • AI itself: not on the accountability chain. AI is a drafting and acceleration tool, not a decision-maker.
Say: "The accountability framing the workshop pushed us toward is the line that makes any AI build defensible. When anyone asks 'who is responsible?' — the answer is always a named human, never the model."

10 Learn-and-listen → feedback → guardrails

The training's Three Levels of Listening (Level 0 my assumptions · Level 1 stated · Level 2 feelings/needs · Level 3 what is possible) reshaped our discovery practice. Concretely:

  • We listened, at Level 2, to what engineers actually feared — not "will AI replace me" but "will my manager think I didn't do the work?"
  • We listened, at Level 3, to what they thought was possible — "if I owned the AI quality guidelines, what would they look like?"
  • Out of that came the guardrails — Coding Agents Policy, AI usage disclosure, the Brown Bag sessions where teams share what worked and what didn't.

Honest answer — is AI productivity at scale real?

11 AI productivity at scale — real or myth? Our position

Answered honestly, with internal evidence and external data — not with hand-waving.

The honest answer from 2026 external data: productivity gains are real where deployment is mature, mostly myth where stuck in pilot — and most organisations are stuck in pilot.

  • Real: Q1 2026 surveys (McKinsey, Salesforce, Slack, Anthropic enterprise telemetry) converge on 6.1–7.2 hours saved per knowledge worker per week. 79% of organisations use AI agents to some degree; 66% report measurable productivity improvements.
  • Myth at scale: March 2026 survey — 78% of enterprises have AI agent pilots; under 15% reach production. McKinsey: 92% plan more AI investment, but only 1% say they are mature in deployment. PwC: 75% of AI economic gains captured by 20% of companies — uneven distribution.
  • The bottleneck isn't the model. Frontier AI quality is not the constraint. The constraint is "everything between a frontier model and a measurable outcome" — evaluation frameworks, production monitoring, accountability chains. Exactly the work the workshop pushed us toward.

Where we sit, evidenced not asserted:

  • We are past pilot in 3 places: Vulnerability Sentinel (Planting) — production PRs · Sonar-fixer (Sustainability Mobile) — PR #1347 shipped · Lalitkumar's Agrobiodiversity operating principle — published cross-team artefact. That is what "past pilot" looks like.
  • We have measured before/after data: Sahil's AI Integration Test 1 produced Manoj's enthusiastic written reaction (the deck's strongest single endorsement). Lalitkumar's RN v77 case study: 12 SP → 3-4 SP, 67–75% reduction, 8-9 SP freed.
  • We have the structural practice that separates the 20% from the 80%: Brown Bag forum sharing what worked AND what didn't (P4); Agent-discussion cadence (P3★); guardrails published before usage (P1, Slide 9). These are the practices the McKinsey "mature deployers" share.

Our position to Andre / Uday: The productivity-at-scale story is real when the leadership scaffolding is real — and the scaffolding is what we have built since the training. Where we have not yet shipped, we have a route, not a hope.

Say: "We are not claiming a finished story. We are claiming we are running in the lane the data says actually works — past-pilot artefacts, measured before/after, structural sharing practice. That is what mature deployment looks like at this stage, and it is what we will keep doing."
Close — what we are learning, and what we are asking for

12 What we are learning — honestly (and what we did not know we did not know)

Things the training-shaped approach surfaced that we would not have seen without it — the workshop's deliberate value: making the "unknown unknowns" visible. Naming them is Growth Partner mode required practice.

What we knew we knew before the training: tools exist; some teams want to use them; we should run pilots.

What we knew we didn't know: which pilots scale; what governance looks like; how to measure impact.

What we did NOT know we did not know (surfaced by the training):

  • Senior engineers' resistance is not Luddism — it is identity protection. Before the training, we read it as resistance to change. The Hofstede Masculinity lens reframed it: senior engineers protect years of invested craft. We adjust by leading with "AI mastery is the new craft", not "AI is more efficient." We would not have seen this re-frame on our own.
  • "What didn't work" is the right Brown Bag content, not the polite version. Before the training, we assumed shared-success was the format. The Trust-Acceptance element pushed us to surface failure-content explicitly — and engagement is qualitatively different. The room treats it as professional development, not internal marketing.
  • Cross-functional ownership of agents shifts who carries the agenda. Before the training, we assumed engineering would lead agent design. The RACI-with-one-Accountable framing made us see: where BA + Tech Lead + Engineering co-own the cadence (Planting agent track), design discussions are about use cases and outcomes; where engineering owns alone, they are about implementation. The latter ships faster and travels further. This was not on our radar.
  • Asking "what is the question we are afraid to ask?" changes the room. The workbook's VALUE-Anticipate framing is the move that has produced the most surprising 1:1 conversations — and several pilot pivots. Uncomfortable, not optional. We did not know we needed this prompt until the training gave it to us.
  • The training's biggest gift is not a tool — it is a vocabulary. "Loyal Soldier → Growth Partner," "thresholded gate vs always-on gate," "Invisible Influencer," "anticipate and ask," "small-and-deep beats uniform-and-thin." Each phrase makes a previously invisible pattern nameable and discussable. That is the workshop's largest unmeasured outcome.

13 Where we need alignment, and what we commit to next

Where we are seeking sponsor alignment from Andre & Uday:
